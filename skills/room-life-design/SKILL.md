# Room Life Design

Design first for visual comfort, emotional release, and a vlog-ready sense of lived experience. The primary product test is whether the first frame makes the viewer want to enter and stay. The room must remain physically believable and minimally usable, but conventional ergonomics, routine convenience, cleaning effort, and hotel-like openness are not primary goals. Treat style labels and decorative objects as downstream choices.

## Installed-skill default

When this skill is invoked for a bedroom makeover, a room-photo upload with no extra preferences is sufficient: analyze, choose capacity-matched functions, generate with the host's available image-editing tool, inspect, and deliver the resulting image with a short explanation. Do not ask routine intake questions or stop at a prompt when image editing is available. An explicit request for analysis or a prompt only must remain analysis or prompt only. Honor user-supplied functions and retention constraints; offer a concise function-selection question only when the user requests a guided choice. Ask for missing input only when the photo is unusable or a critical ambiguity prevents a believable result. If the host cannot generate/edit images, disclose that limitation rather than claiming a render was produced. Installing this skill does not grant tools, credentials, or a model.

## Non-negotiable reasoning rules

1. Audit the evidence before interpreting it. Do not assume that filenames, folder order, or multiple views represent before/after chronology. Mark each case as paired, after-only, before-only, ambiguous, duplicate, or empty.
2. Count independent rooms, not folders or photographs. Treat repeated rooms and alternate views as one case with more evidence.
3. Separate three claim types: visible fact, plausible inference, and user-dependent recommendation. Never invent hidden construction, budget, dimensions, or a missing “before”. Also separate **transformation evidence** from **target-aesthetic evidence**: a high-engagement finished room may be a strong visual target even when it cannot prove which change produced the result.
4. Apply the user's low-friction capacity policy: sleep is mandatory; analyze the image and reserve the bed, fixed openings, and a minimally workable route; then choose only capacity-approved auxiliary scenes, honoring explicit customer requirements. If the user requests a guided choice, state capacity before asking one concise function question. Never ask the customer to estimate dimensions or choose a scene that has not passed the capacity check.
5. Design relationships, not shopping lists. A chair becomes a usable scene only when its placement, light, nearby surface, reach, boundary, and circulation make the activity believable.
6. Judge scene capacity again after temporary obstruction is removed or contained. Do not let boxes, suitcases, unstable piles, redundant furniture, or an unhelpful large wardrobe make a capable room seem permanently too small. Movable storage may be removed, replaced, or redistributed when visual impact and selected scenes improve; never invent capacity by changing architecture or trusting wide-angle distortion.
7. Require functional closure for every named scene: posture/seat, anchor, activity resource, reachable support, appropriate light/power, and a usable access envelope. A rug, chair, or bookcase alone is not a scene.
8. For a new customer photo, infer bed placement, style and fitting auxiliary functions unless the user supplies preferences. A photo-only makeover proceeds automatically. Do not routinely ask about dimensions, sleeper count, functions, bed placement, style, ordinary movable items, or plant count. Follow [references/customer-intake.md](references/customer-intake.md) for guided-choice requests or critical missing information.
9. If no bed is visible, plan with a 1.8 m × 2.0 m bed; if a bed is visible, retain its apparent size/type but allow it to move or rotate. Before fixing the layout, explicitly compare the photographed position, both 90-degree axes, a 180-degree head/foot reversal, sliding along every viable wall, and every two-wall corner. In ordinary bedrooms prefer the candidate whose head and one long side touch walls and which releases the most valuable residual corner or continuous footprint for complete auxiliary scenes. Only a genuinely large room may center or float the bed while retaining complete auxiliary scenes.
10. Preserve architecture, openings, air conditioner, and the existing sleep surface's apparent size/type. The bed frame may change and the bed may rotate, relocate, or become a wall-aligned daybed. A large wardrobe is not mandatory: preserve it only when it supports the chosen image; otherwise remove, replace, or redistribute storage. Other movable items may be freely replaced for the visual result unless the customer explicitly asks to keep them.
11. Do not default to erasing meaningful life evidence. Retain or reinterpret active-use and identity/collection objects when they strengthen the render; simplify functional noise and replace generic or visually conflicting movable items.
12. Preserve useful negative space without making an emotional void. “Lived-in” may be tight, layered, and object-rich. Require only plausible entry, posture, basic operation, and exit for selected activities; allow single-side bed access, sequential overlap of movement space, open storage, plush objects, and high-maintenance styling. Reject only impossible or blocked use, not inconvenience or cleaning effort.
13. Infer a compatible material/style family from fixed surfaces, but treat existing daylight as evidence rather than the required final mood. Pale wood can support cream/forest or a youthful bookish retro room with dark checks, brown wood, red accents, plants, and mixed objects; gray marble can be softened beyond sterile monochrome. When the customer gives no time-of-day preference, default to a bright warm interior in soft daytime/late-afternoon context: gentle exterior base light, broad diffuse warm ambient light, local activity pools, and open readable shadows. Warm artificial light should shape the mood without suppressing all daylight. Follow [references/lighting-model.md](references/lighting-model.md); blue hour is an optional mood, not the default.
14. In customer renders, avoid large unexplained blank wall fields. Activate visible walls at multiple heights with resident-relevant posters, photographs, felt/pin boards, shelves, textiles, hanging objects, lamps, or plants, using irregular groupings rather than a formal grid.
15. In customer renders, include at least three visible, plausibly placed plants unless the customer opts out. Vary scale and placement, connect them to scenes or daylight, and keep them clear of routes, work surfaces, curtains, and sleep access.
16. Separate order by scale: keep macro layout and access orderly, allow meso-scale scenes to overlap, and preserve micro-scale irregularity. For this user's object-rich target, require distributed evidence of ongoing life: several relevant surfaces in use, stored/current/temporarily placed objects, irregular stacks and partial visual overlap. A wrinkled duvet and one open book do not establish lived-in density. Use the activity-state map and rejection checks in [references/comfort-density.md](references/comfort-density.md); coherence comes from relationships, not universal alignment or objects all returned to assigned places.
17. Treat photography as part of the product. Choose the time of day, exposure, foreground obstruction, depth sequence, and viewpoint for emotional pull rather than neutral documentation. When several scenes exist, prefer a hero view that lets them unfold from foreground to background, and use a close/detail view when supported to make the most inviting corner tactile. The viewpoint may change, but room geometry, fixed openings, and the chosen furniture layout must remain consistent; never widen or rebuild the room through perspective.

## Design sequence

1. Inspect the room before asking: lock fixed constraints, identify the existing sleep surface and movable storage, classify other objects, compare occupied versus recovered capacity, reserve a 1.8 m × 2.0 m bed if empty, enumerate both 90-degree axes, the 180-degree head/foot reversal, wall-axis slides and all plausible wall corners, and choose a visually ambitious style route compatible with the fixed surfaces. Do not stop at the photographed orientation merely because it already contains a bed or wardrobe.
2. State the chosen functions briefly and continue using [references/customer-intake.md](references/customer-intake.md). If preferences are absent, choose automatically; if supplied, honor them. Only wait for a function selection when the user explicitly requests guided choice.
3. Restate the inferred bed/style and selected functions, then compare viable bed-and-scene candidates. First reject candidates that block fixed openings or make a selected activity impossible. Test whether slimmer anchors—such as a shallow desk, armless push-under chair, low open storage, floor seat, beanbag, or compact side surface—can close each selected function independently before reducing the scene count or combining it with the bed. Among the remaining candidates, prefer the layout that creates the strongest emotional composition and densest complete life system, even when bed access is one-sided, passages are compact, storage is redistributed, or movement envelopes overlap sequentially.
4. Choose the smallest intervention that changes the root problem: reroute an obstacle, reconnect an active-use object, form an overlapping cluster, create a soft or low boundary, improve light topology, activate a vertical surface, or strengthen personal evidence.
5. Test candidate layouts in this order: fixed architecture/openings; minimum activity plausibility; independent closure of every customer-selected scene; emotional composition and relational density; then optional convenience. A work scene fails only when a person cannot plausibly enter, sit, work, and leave; a traditional ergonomic office chair, generous commercial clearance, and simultaneous circulation are not benefits unless the customer requests long-hour ergonomic work. For multiple selected functions, prefer separate complete micro-scenes when a furniture-envelope or bed-placement change makes them possible. Use a bed as the lounge/daybed only after those independent candidates fail or when the brief explicitly favors bed-centered life. Treat a purposeless large rug or floor patch as a missed-capacity warning.
6. Stage recommendations in priority order: no-buy/rearrangement, low-cost atmosphere, furniture/lighting, then structural work only if justified.
7. If a render is requested, work in two passes: establish a plausible transformation plan, then apply the high-acceptance aesthetic grammar and comfort-density model. Preserve geometry and fixed elements explicitly. Describe zoning tier, bed orientation, independent versus compound scenes, activity chains, overlaps, density transitions, boundaries, circulation, four-layer bright-warm lighting, material/silhouette variation, depth layers, active surfaces, wall activation, identity, and at least three plausible plant placements before naming generic decorative props.
8. Before prompting, translate comfort density into an activity-state map from [references/comfort-density.md](references/comfort-density.md), naming which surfaces are in use and how their objects overlap or extend to adjacent scenes. When supported, give the renderer the target room plus one high-engagement visual reference matched to both capacity and the requested density. Use a second only for one explicitly named missing quality. Reference images guide mood and relational density, never source-room architecture or exact possessions.
9. Inspect a generated result first with a three-second invitation test: would the hero frame make the intended customer want to enter and stay? Then check fixed-geometry consistency, minimum activity plausibility, independent/compound scene closure, broad warm coverage, readable interior midtones and shadow detail, and the over-completion rejection test. Trace whether a person can approach, adopt the posture, use the activity, and leave. Compare the actual activity-state map and selected density reference, not just object presence: do not accept a tidy composition merely because the duvet has folds and a book is open. If density fails while layout and light pass, correct object states, overlap and scene connections without resetting the layout or darkening the room. Do not reward conventional office formality, neutral daylight, compactness avoidance, two-sided bed access, or easy upkeep.

## Growth loop

When the user adds examples and asks the skill to learn from them:

1. Build contact sheets with `scripts/build_case_contact_sheets.py` when side-by-side inspection helps. The script is read-only toward source images.
2. Analyze each independent room with the common method; do not change the rubric midway.
3. Score its role on two axes: transformation evidence and target-aesthetic evidence. Record user-supplied engagement provenance when available without inventing exact metrics.
4. For each existing transformation principle and aesthetic pattern, record whether the new case supports, narrows, contradicts, or does not test it.
5. Promote a transformation principle only when it repeats across paired cases or has strong spatial reasoning. Promote an aesthetic pattern when it repeats across independent, audience-validated finished rooms; preserve minority patterns as compatible style families rather than errors.
6. Update the evidence-base version, source counts, case notes, confidence, aesthetic families, exceptions, and unresolved questions. Update the text index for retained representative images so future customer runs can shortlist without reopening the whole library. Do not append universal rules for every attractive image or silently update the library during ordinary generation.

The final standard is not “does the room contain cozy objects?” It is whether the first frame presents a believable, emotionally absorbing life world that the customer immediately wants to enter: visually comfortable, personal, vlog-ready, and logically usable at a minimum. Maintenance effort, conventional office ergonomics, neutral daylight, generous circulation, and formal tidiness are optional—not success criteria.
