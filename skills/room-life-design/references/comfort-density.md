# Comfort density: order without emotional distance

Read this reference for customer-room generation and for critiquing a result that feels tidy, formal, generic, old-fashioned, or emotionally distant.

## Core correction

Do not translate “coherent” into “uniform”, or “usable” into “cleared”. The target is:

- **Macro order:** architecture, anchor furniture, fixed access, safety, and minimum activity sequences make sense.
- **Meso overlap:** life scenes connect and partially share rugs, shelves, lights, side surfaces, or visual motifs instead of becoming isolated furniture sets.
- **Micro irregularity:** books, bedding, art, work tools, textiles, and personal objects vary in alignment, height, age, material, and state of use.

If all three scales are orderly, the room becomes staged and distant. If none is orderly, it becomes unusable. Keep only the macro logic needed for plausibility while allowing meso overlap and micro irregularity. Tightness, one-sided bed access, open storage, abundant textiles, plush toys, and regular restyling effort are acceptable when they improve the emotional image.

## Classify existing objects before removing them

### 1. Active-use evidence — usually retain and reorganize

Examples: current books, headphones, keyboard, notebook, cup, chargers in use, blanket, bedside items, plant-care tools. Place them where the activity occurs and allow a believable in-use state.

### 2. Identity and collection — retain selectively and layer

Examples: photographs, toys, records, art, hobby tools, souvenirs, favorite books, distinctive lamps. These make the room belong to a person. Mix hanging, leaning, open, closed, high, and low placements rather than turning them into a formal display grid.

### 3. Generic decoration — use sparingly

Examples: anonymous framed prints, generic candles, neutral vases, decorative books, duplicate plants. They can support composition but should not replace specific identity.

### 4. Functional noise or hazard — remove, contain, or reroute

Examples: completely blocked fixed openings, unstable stacks, redundant packaging, broken objects, hygiene problems, unsafe open flames, uncontrolled cables, or items that make a selected activity impossible. This is the main category to simplify. Do not classify an object as noise merely because it increases cleaning effort or makes the room visually dense.

Do not delete categories 1 and 2 merely because the photo looks busy. The objective is not a blank surface; it is a surface whose visible objects explain its use.

## How comfortable irregularity is constructed

- Concentrate detail at lived-in edges: bed side, head/foot shelf, desk return, window ledge, reading corner, or low cabinet.
- Let clusters overlap through a shared rug edge, low shelf, lamp pool, plant silhouette, or repeated color. Avoid a bed set on the left and a desk set on the right with an emotionally empty gap.
- Mix furniture origins while maintaining echoes. Two woods may coexist if their warmth repeats elsewhere; frames need not match; one vintage or playful object can interrupt a coordinated set.
- Vary height and orientation: horizontal books beside vertical books, one leaning frame beside hanging work, a draped textile crossing a hard edge, plants with different silhouettes.
- Keep several relevant surfaces visibly active across the room, scaled to its capacity and the requested density. A notebook, headphones, cup, controller, current book, or charging device belongs at its place of use, not necessarily its storage position. Do not cap life evidence at one or two token surfaces.
- Make soft materials respond to use: compressed cushions, imperfect bedding, folds, layered rugs, a throw that is actually reachable. Avoid perfectly centered pillows and showroom-tight bedding.
- Preserve visual time. The room should look accumulated and adjusted over time rather than purchased as one matching collection.
- Allow small surprises tied to the resident: an unusual lamp, toy, print, stool, textile, or object with a different era or shape.
- Vary silhouettes as deliberately as color: place soft slouching volumes, round or arched objects, vertical hobby objects, leaf shapes and draped textiles against the room's rectangular bed, desk and walls. A composition made only from clean rectangles can remain emotionally formal even when it contains warm colors.

## Distributed life, not a tidy room with a wrinkled duvet

For this user's preferred object-rich image, the target is a room that appears lived in over time and recently used. This is a visual brief, not proof that a reference photograph is unstaged. Increasing quantity alone does not work: a shelf full of evenly aligned books can still look formal.

Use these concrete mechanisms where they fit:

- **Different states:** let a coherent object family appear stored, currently used and temporarily set down. For example, books on a shelf, a small offset reading pile beside a seat and a current volume on the bed imply use across time. Do not duplicate the same object or repeat an identical vignette at every surface.
- **Several active surfaces:** connect the bed edge, side table, shelf top, desk if selected, or window ledge to plausible activities. Each remains usable; not every surface needs to be covered.
- **Mixed arrangement:** combine upright, horizontal, leaning and casually resting objects, uneven spacing and varied stack heights. Keep some ordinary alignment so the result does not become mechanically scattered or universally diagonal.
- **Partial overlap:** allow a plant to obscure part of a cabinet, a lamp arm to cross the wall/shelf layer, or a textile to cross a hard edge. Full furniture silhouettes need not be exposed. This is visual overlap, not physical interpenetration or concealment of impossible access.
- **Life extending between scenes:** keep sleep, lounge and work functionally distinct when selected, but let books, textiles, repeated colors and reachable support connect their edges. Functional zoning does not require visually isolated furniture islands.
- **Varied visual texture:** use compatible differences in wood, print scale, woven/knitted/plush surfaces and small personal color accents. Do not repeat one neutral palette and material on everything, or require the reference's exact plaid, toys or posters.

### Pre-render activity-state map

Alongside the functional scene map, briefly map: **surface → resident activity → object state → neighboring relationship**. For example, shelf → reading collection → upright books plus a short uneven pile → current book on the adjacent lounge table. Choose a coherent resident story without asking extra intake questions. Multiple supported surfaces should carry it; no universal object count, clutter percentage, or fixed number of props applies.

Match a visual reference for this density specifically. A reference with the correct scene count but sparse, fully arranged surfaces is insufficient for a request for more lived-in irregularity. Do not inherit its functions, architecture or exact possessions merely to gain density.

### Prompt block

> Show a room lived in over time and recently used, with several activity-linked surfaces active at once. Mix stored collections, objects currently in use and a few temporarily resting objects. Let small book stacks, textiles, leaning pieces and plants form uneven, overlapping clusters that extend from storage to their places of use. Preserve some ordinary alignment and quieter intervals; keep stable supports and plausible activity access. Vary materials and small personal color accents. Do not return every object to a designated display position, isolate every furniture silhouette, or substitute one open book and a wrinkled duvet for distributed life. Keep the chosen room geometry, functions and bright warm lighting unchanged when correcting density.

## Build a coherent identity scaffold when the resident is unknown

Do not solve missing user information with generic candles, identical plants, and anonymous framed prints. Use a coherent but reversible identity scaffold:

- a hobby/collection thread, such as books, music, drawing, film, plants, or photography;
- wearable/display evidence, such as a hat, tote, textile, pinboard, or small collection;
- playful character, such as plush objects, an unusual lamp, graphic mat, or unexpected vintage piece;
- active-use evidence, such as open books, headphones, notebook, mug, controller, or instrument placed at their real scenes.

These are conceptual roles, not a one-object-per-role checklist or a requirement to add every category. Let a compatible story recur across the room with varied scale, silhouette and use state. Do not invent personal facts about the customer or copy a creator's exact guitar, poster, toy collection, or personal photographs.

## Avoid the “older formal bedroom” shortcut

Dark wood, plaid, pleated lamps, thick taupe curtains, traditional rugs, and framed art can each be warm. When all appear together in matching, aligned form, they can read as conservative and formal. To keep a bookish or retro room youthful:

- combine dark textiles with lighter or playful pieces rather than a complete matching bed set;
- use mixed small/washed rugs or a less formal large rug;
- mix one structured lamp with a softer paper or sculptural light;
- use an irregular wall layer rather than a framed grid;
- include current-use objects and one personal/playful cue;
- retain cool/neutral ambient variation and distinct material colors rather than grading everything brown.

## Prompt-language correction

Avoid repeatedly instructing the renderer to “simplify”, “restrain”, “edit”, “unify”, “clear”, or “remove clutter”. These words accumulate into a showroom prior.

Prefer positive instructions:

- retain meaningful daily-use and collection objects;
- consolidate only obstructive or visually unrelated noise;
- keep minimum entry/use/exit sequences plausible while allowing tight lived-in edges;
- create connected, partially overlapping clusters;
- use echoes rather than matching sets;
- preserve active surfaces and natural textile states;
- keep a high but legible density near anchors and a lower density along the route.

## Over-completion rejection test

Reject or revise a generated room when several of these are true:

- every large edge is parallel and every object appears centered;
- all wood, frames, lamps, and textiles look purchased as one set;
- bed and work/lounge areas read as separate furniture packages;
- a large rug or empty floor becomes a formal blank stage;
- all books and objects are upright, evenly spaced, or purely decorative;
- nearly all objects remain in assigned storage/display positions despite a request for an object-rich lived-in room;
- one open book or blanket fold carries all the life evidence while other surfaces remain reset;
- object count is high but placement states, cluster heights and silhouettes stay uniformly arranged;
- every scene and wall collage is an isolated composition with no cross-surface visual connection;
- bedding is hotel-tight and pillows form a symmetrical wall;
- personal items could belong to anyone;
- lighting is uniformly warm rather than gathered around activities;
- every object is rectilinear and similarly finished, so the scene lacks a soft or playful silhouette break;
- the image feels fully “finished” with no trace of an activity before or after the photograph;
- the room is usable and attractive but the viewer cannot imagine touching, moving, or leaving anything in it.

As a diagnostic, mentally discount bedding wrinkles and the token open book: if the remaining object arrangement still reads like a showroom, the requested life density has not been achieved. Also reject the opposite failure: uniformly scattered objects, unstable piles, blocked posture/access or collections with no relation to any activity. Reference density is qualitative, not a quantity target.

When these symptoms appear, first change object states, reconnect several active surfaces, loosen selected alignments and introduce believable partial overlap. Add activity-linked objects only where the comparison still lacks substance; do not scatter filler. If layout and bright-warm lighting already pass, preserve them during this correction. The same test applies after a bedding-only or lighting-only edit so polish does not erase existing life evidence.
