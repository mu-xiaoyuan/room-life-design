## Principles supported by the collection

### P1. Diagnose the missing layer before adding objects — high confidence

The strongest paired cases (3, 4/9, 6, 11, 14) required a change in anchor placement, spatial mass, or a credible life scene. Room 2 is the important counterexample: its sleep/work structure already worked, so light, textiles, wall activation, and personal evidence were sufficient. Therefore “scene before props” is a diagnostic order, not a command to rezone every room.

### P2. Use one primary scene and only scale-appropriate auxiliary scenes — high confidence

Large rooms 1, 5/8, 6, and 16 support full lounge or work scenes. Small rooms 7, 11, and 13 show that a bed-centered nest or one micro-corner can be enough. The correct question is not “How many zones?” but “Which activities can be made believable without sacrificing access and calm?”

### P3. Completeness comes from relationships, not item count — high confidence

Across the finished rooms, useful clusters repeat: seat + reachable surface + appropriate light + soft boundary; bed + reachable light + storage/surface + textile layer; desk + task light + storage/display + window or wall context. An isolated chair or plant used to fill a gap rarely creates an activity.

### P4. Boundaries are useful when they remain proportional — high confidence

Room 6 uses a low shelf; 5/8 uses a large rug; 4/9 uses orientation and furniture edges; 11 and 13 use low furniture and pools of light. A boundary need not be a divider. In small rooms, a strong physical separator can obstruct circulation, while a rug, light pool, or orientation change may be enough.

### P5. Negative space and circulation are designed assets — medium-high confidence

Room 10 improves atmosphere while retaining a useful central route. Several denser rooms look attractive despite higher upkeep. Empty floor is not automatically a defect, but in this product it earns its place only when it supports fixed access, a real activity, or deliberate visual relief—not cleaning convenience or generic spaciousness alone.

### P6. Light topology and capture time work together — high confidence for the product image

The compelling rooms distribute light by activity and height: desk lamp, bedside lamp, floor lamp, shelf/accent glow, plus retained shadow. Paired rooms 2, 3, 6, 11, 14, and live case A visibly strengthen local lighting. Live case A supports warm/cool contrast as one useful mood, not blue hour as a universal default. The later four-view comparison and explicit user feedback narrow the preference to bright warm mixed-light interiors: neutral/gently cool exterior base light, broad warm ambient coverage, local activity pools and readable shadows. Capture choices belong to the product, but photographs do not establish actual lamp outputs, color temperatures or light-source contribution ratios. Neither neutral daylight nor darker cinematic grading is automatically better.

### P7. Warmth is tactile and relational, not a beige palette — high confidence

Rooms 3 and 7 use dark blue and black; 5/8 uses olive and deep brown; 2 uses navy; 13 remains pale. They feel comfortable through fabric volume, wood, rugs, curtains, plants, local light, and value contrast. All-cream styling is one option, not the mechanism.

### P8. Vertical surfaces and height variation create depth efficiently — medium-high confidence

Rooms 2, 3, 4/9, 11, and 14 activate walls or windows at several heights with curtains, art, shelves, plants, and lamps. This increases visual stops without consuming as much floor area. Keep safety, dust, and visual overload in view.

### P9. Identity should be specific enough to imply a person — medium-high confidence

Books, a guitar, record player, workstation, plants, toys, photographs, and collected art work best when tied to a plausible resident activity. Generic “cozy props” can reproduce the appearance without the ownership feeling. Because customer intake should remain minimal, infer a coherent reversible identity scaffold when the resident has not supplied one: a hobby anchor, wearable/display cue, playful cue, and active-use cue, without copying a creator's exact possessions.

### P10. Controlled irregularity is a result, not a percentage — medium confidence

Natural bedding, leaning art, mixed book orientation, and reachable objects soften showroom formality. The prior “10–20% clutter” formulation is unjustifiably precise. The product boundary is whether objects support a legible emotional hierarchy and do not make a selected activity impossible; cleanability is not a visual acceptance gate.

### P11. Photographic comfort and residential comfort must be separated — high confidence as an analysis rule

Warm white balance, evening darkness, candles, wide angles, styled bedding, and temporary object placement recur in the collection. They cannot prove ergonomics, storage adequacy, airflow, safety, or maintenance. For this visual-first product, however, legitimate capture choices—especially blue-hour cool ambience, local warm pools, viewpoint and foreground depth—are part of the desired output rather than penalties. Only geometry distortion or logically impossible use remains disqualifying.

### P12. Independent micro-scenes can exceed apparent compact-room capacity — high confidence from live case A

Capacity must be judged after a full bed-position search and after shrinking furniture envelopes. Live case A fits a complete sleep scene, a shallow window office and a separate lounge by sliding the bed toward the front/entrance, using a push-under armless chair, moving storage into low open cubbies, and spending the released corner on a soft lounge cluster. A bed/daybed compound scene is useful, but it must not become the default shortcut when a distinct emotionally attractive lounge can be closed.

## Corrections to the initial dialogue model

The initial discussion correctly identified life scenes, connection, local light, soft materials, identity, and controlled imperfection. The expanded evidence narrows several statements:

- As an evidence claim, replace “every successful bedroom must create at least two obvious living zones” with “the examples support one primary scene plus scale-appropriate auxiliary scenes”. For customer generation, follow the later low-friction intake policy: sleep stays mandatory, while the customer selects only the analyzed number of capacity-approved auxiliary functions.
- Replace “every zone needs at least three objects” with “every scene needs the relationships required for its activity”; the exact count is irrelevant.
- Replace “do not place all furniture along walls” with “do not leave furniture unrelated”; perimeter layouts can be excellent in small rooms.
- Replace “use furniture to create boundaries” with “use the least obstructive boundary that makes the scene legible”.
- Replace “add a dark anchor” with “create sufficient value and material depth”; a dark anchor is one method.
- Replace “avoid lighting the whole room” with “provide adequate ambient/task light, then use local pools and shadow for atmosphere”.
- Replace “10–20% controlled mess” with a qualitative standard based on activity meaning, emotional hierarchy, and minimum physical plausibility rather than cleaning ease.
- Treat strong asymmetry as optional. The real problem is impersonal, rigid composition, not symmetry itself.

## Correction from customer-room generation trials

The second live trial produced a plausible sleep/work room that preserved the source architecture but felt too formal, aligned, clean, and emotionally distant. It combined matching dark walnut, navy plaid, a traditional rug, thick taupe curtains, pleated lighting, orderly framed art, cleared surfaces, and isolated bed/work zones. This exposed a translation failure: the evidence base described connected high-density rooms, while the generation prompt repeatedly asked to simplify, restrain, unify, clear, and remove.

Revisions established by this failure:

- Treat the curated rooms as direct visual references when the image tool supports multiple inputs; textual summaries alone lose object overlap, mixed origins, imperfect alignment, and temporal accumulation.
- Separate macro order, meso overlap, and micro irregularity. Only the first should be consistently tidy.
- Classify existing objects before removal. Preserve active-use evidence and identity/collection; limit generic decoration; remove or contain functional noise and hazards.
- Replace “clear central space” with “maintain a legible route and connected density transitions”. A shared rug, low table, active shelf edge, or lamp pool may bridge scenes without blocking circulation.
- Replace palette uniformity with material and color echoes. Avoid making woods, frames, lamps, bedding, and rugs look purchased as one set.
- Treat matching dark wood + plaid + heavy neutral curtains + traditional rug + formal framed grid as a possible older-formal shortcut, not the default meaning of bookish warmth.
- Add an over-completion rejection test. A render can pass geometry and usability checks while still failing emotional proximity.

## Correction from functional-zoning trial

A later live trial improved the visual tone—more youthful, irregular, and lived-in—but exposed a separate planning failure. The source photograph looked congested because of a person, boxes, suitcases, folded furniture, and unstable piles. The prompt judged capacity from that obstructed state and explicitly limited the redesign to sleep and work. Once the temporary noise was removed, the render revealed enough floor area for a third rest/reading scene, but left it as a large rug. A bookcase was placed near the bed/window for composition even though no independent reading seat, landing surface, or usable browsing relationship served it.

Revisions established by this failure:

- Compare current occupied capacity with **recovered capacity** after temporary functional noise is removed or contained. Do not let photographed congestion prematurely forbid an auxiliary scene.
- Do not turn the correction into a universal three-zone rule. The later user-defined product policy requires mandatory sleep plus customer-selected auxiliary scenes according to capacity; additional specialist scenes still depend on fixed furniture, essential storage, access envelopes, plausible scale, and resident needs.
- Require functional closure for every named scene: posture or seat, anchor, activity resources, reachable support, appropriate light/power, and a usable entry/exit envelope.
- Place storage according to the activity it serves. A reading bookcase must be browsable from a real reading scene; a desk chair does not automatically count as that seat.
- Treat rugs as boundaries or connectors, never as evidence that a scene exists. A large rug or floor patch without an activity, route, access, or deliberate relief purpose is a missed-capacity warning.
- Make the render audit compare the promised scene count with the visible usable scene count before evaluating decorative warmth.

## Correction from office-clearance trial

A later customer-room render contained the requested bed and office furniture, but the desk chair was visually trapped between the bed and desk. The prompt had favored window daylight and preserved much of the photographed bed orientation without comparing a 90-degree rotation or other viable wall-corner candidates. This exposed a second functional translation failure: an anchor can fit while its resident and moving parts cannot.

Revisions established by this failure:

- Preserve an existing bed's apparent size/type, not its photographed location or orientation. Compare current, rotated, slid and viable two-wall-corner candidates before choosing.
- Reserve a visible human-use sequence for every scene. For an office this includes approach, knee/seat posture, desk operation and a plausible exit—not only the desk rectangle.
- Treat fixed architecture and total activity impossibility as hard gates. Compact chair movement and a sequentially shared route are acceptable; among passing candidates optimize emotional composition and intentional capture mood.
- Prefer a bed orientation that recovers a complete selected scene over one that creates purposeless residual strips. Do not automatically choose the candidate with the widest circulation.
- In post-render review, visually trace the resident's action sequence. Furniture presence is not proof of a usable scene.

## Blue-hour independent-scene correction from live case A

The direct comparison between the same source bedroom, the AI sleep/office/bed-lounge render, and the creator's two finished views exposed five remaining biases:

1. **The layout search was incomplete.** It tested rotation and corner placement but did not sufficiently test 180-degree head/foot reversal and sliding the bed along its wall toward the entrance/front. The creator used that move to release the most valuable residual corner.
2. **The AI compounded leisure into the bed too early.** The creator first protected an independent lounge with a genuine relaxed posture, foot support, rug, side storage, local light, plants and identity cues. A compound bed/daybed should be fallback or a deliberate bed-centered choice, not a convenient way to claim an extra function.
3. **The AI treated conventional office furniture as quality.** A substantial rolling office chair and generous sitting geometry consumed visual and spatial capacity. The creator used a shallow desk and armless push-under wood chair; minimum believable use was enough, and the saved envelope funded a more emotionally valuable lounge.
4. **The AI treated neutral daylight as quality.** The creator's cool blue exterior/window field and warm 2700–3200 K interior pools create refuge, depth and a filmic vlog frame. Natural daytime color is not an advantage under a first-glance emotional brief.
5. **The AI's identity and silhouettes were too generic.** Laptop, books, plants and a pinboard formed a competent but generic set. The creator combined leather-like slouching seating, a footstool, rattan, wood, soft rug, leaf canopies, a vertical instrument, hat, toys, active desk objects and irregular wall art into one accumulated resident story.

The resulting product rule is a **three-second invitation gate**: before praising circulation, ergonomics, daylight neutrality or ease of cleaning, ask whether the hero image immediately makes the intended customer want to enter and stay. Those practical qualities become tie-breakers only after minimum physical logic passes.

## User-defined product zoning and warmth policy

The evidence base remains descriptive, but the customer-image product now has explicit user-defined defaults that supersede the earlier conservative generation behavior:

- Every generated bedroom must contain a complete sleep scene.
- After inspecting a new customer photo, infer bed, capacity, fixed-item retention and style. Honor supplied functions or choose them automatically when absent; photo-only makeover requests proceed without a routine question. Guided function selection is available on explicit request. Ask otherwise only for critical missing input or an unresolved conflict in explicit requirements, following [customer-intake.md](customer-intake.md).
- Do not ask for room dimensions or sleeper count. For an empty room reserve a 1.8 m × 2.0 m bed; if a bed is visible, keep its apparent size/type.
- In ordinary bedrooms default the bed to a two-wall corner to consolidate useful floor. Only a genuinely large room may center or float it while preserving complete selected scenes.
- Before fixing that bed position, compare the photographed position, both 90-degree axes, a 180-degree head/foot reversal, sliding along every viable wall and each viable wall corner. Preserve mattress size/type but allow relocation and frame replacement; reject impossible candidates, then choose the candidate that releases the most valuable complete residual corner and strongest emotional composition rather than the widest passage.
- Preserve architecture, windows, air conditioner and the existing sleep footprint/type. A large wardrobe and other movable furniture may be removed, replaced or redistributed when they conflict with selected functions or the finished visual effect.
- When guided choice is requested, state the credible auxiliary-scene count before asking. Show only concise labels for functions that already fit; do not offer a third scene merely because the customer might want one.
- Choose style automatically using fixed surfaces as compatibility anchors, not commands. Wood supports forest/natural, retro or layered bookish routes; gray marble can begin cool/minimal but must be emotionally softened; pale or yellow-brown wood may also support youthful bookish retro. Prefer a specific high-acceptance identity over safe neutrality.
- Customer renders should not leave major visible wall planes unexplained and blank. Activate them with irregular resident-linked posters, photographs, felt/pin boards, shelves, textiles, hanging objects, lamps, or plant silhouettes rather than formal matching grids.
- Customer renders include at least three visible, varied, plausibly placed plants unless the user opts out. They must relate to daylight or life scenes and must not block circulation or furniture use.
- When the customer does not specify a time of day, default to a bright warm interior with soft daytime/late-afternoon context. Use gentle exterior base light, broad diffuse warm ambient coverage, local activity pools and open readable shadows. Keep colors legible and avoid deep-blue window dominance, crushed blacks, clipped shades and global yellow/orange grading. Blue hour is optional when requested; follow [lighting-model.md](lighting-model.md).
- For multiple selected functions, attempt independent complete micro-scenes first using alternate bed slides, slim furniture and redistributed storage. Use bed/daybed compounding only when independent candidates fail or when a bed-centered nest is explicitly preferred.
- Pass the final image through the three-second invitation gate before conventional ergonomics, circulation generosity, neutral color accuracy or maintenance.

This policy is a product preference, not a claim that every high-engagement reference room uses the same scene count, wall density, or plant count.

## Optimized decision model

Use this order for a target room:

1. **Life brief:** who lives here, what they actually do, and what must remain.
2. **Recovered capacity:** classify temporary obstruction, then compare the photographed state with the plausible usable room after functional noise is contained.
3. **Minimal customer intake:** reserve the inferred bed, fixed openings and minimum use sequences, compute the supported auxiliary count, choose a compatible high-impact style, and honor supplied functions or select them automatically. Use a short function question only for requested guided choice.
4. **Candidate layout search:** compare the photographed position, both 90-degree axes, 180-degree head/foot reversal, every wall-axis slide and viable wall corner; allow movable storage and furniture depth/type to change; reject only candidates that alter architecture or make a selected activity impossible; rank survivors by the value of the complete residual corner they release.
5. **Functional closure:** for every scene, connect approach, posture/seat, anchor, resources, support, light/power, and exit. Attempt independent selected scenes with minimum furniture shells before sequential overlap or compound bed/daybed scenes.
6. **Relationships and boundaries:** form credible clusters using adjacency, reach, orientation, rugs, low storage, curtains, or light.
7. **Bed and void:** default ordinary rooms to the best viable two-wall-corner bed, account for major floor areas, protect fixed openings and minimum use, and reject purposeless residual strips and empty rug stages. Do not reserve emptiness for cleaning or formal spaciousness.
8. **Sensory and camera topology:** choose an intentional capture time; default to the four-layer bright-warm model with gentle exterior base, diffuse warm coverage, local pools and readable shadows; distribute softness, value/silhouette contrast, activated visible walls, at least three plausible plant placements unless opted out, visual heights, triangular depth when available, and a vlog-ready faithful viewpoint.
9. **Identity and use states:** build a coherent resident-specific or reversible story across relevant surfaces. Use the activity-state map in [comfort-density.md](comfort-density.md); vary stored, current and temporary positions, connect scene edges and allow partial overlap. Do not reduce life evidence to one object per role or bedding wrinkles.
10. **Three-second invitation and minimum reality:** first test whether the viewer wants to enter and stay, then test fixed geometry, plausible scale, safety, at least one bed entry, and whether each selected activity can actually be performed. Mention ergonomics, storage volume, maintenance or cost only when the customer asks; never present conventional office furniture or neutral daylight as advantages by default.

Only after these decisions should a style label or shopping list be produced.
