# High-acceptance finished-room aesthetic grammar

Read this reference when the task is to generate, edit, or critique an attractive finished-room image, or when finished-only/high-engagement examples are added to the library.

## What this evidence means

The user selected these images from room-makeover videos with thousands to tens of thousands of likes. Treat that provenance as a useful **audience-acceptance prior**: the finished images represent outcomes that many viewers found attractive enough to endorse. They do not establish which renovation step caused the result, and likes can also reflect the creator, video, thumbnail, trend, or audience.

Maintain two independent labels for a sample:

- **Transformation evidence:** how strongly it supports a claim about what changed and why.
- **Target-aesthetic evidence:** how relevant it is as an example of a desirable final image.

A finished-only room may have low transformation evidence and high target-aesthetic evidence. Do not discard it or describe it as incomplete when the task is learning the desired output appearance.

## Shared visual grammar in the curated finished rooms

### 1. One dominant scene, several subordinate stops

The images remain readable because one large element or scene leads—the bed, lounge, or work/sleep composition—while lamps, plants, shelves, textiles, and art create secondary places for the eye to stop. Avoid both a single empty center and a field in which every object competes equally.

### 2. Asymmetrical balance rather than random asymmetry

The rooms often place different objects on opposing sides, but balance their visual weight through color, height, glow, or mass. “Avoid hotel symmetry” does not mean scattering objects. Use repeated wood tones, recurring checks/stripes, related lampshade shapes, or repeated greenery to make unequal sides feel connected.

### 3. Density has a gradient

High-interest areas cluster around the bed edge, desk, shelf, window, or lounge corner. Routes remain usable, but “quieter” does not mean blank: a low table, rug overlap, active shelf, or shared lamp can bridge scenes. Generate **dense anchors, connected transitions, and a legible route**, not uniform clutter, an empty formal stage, or isolated props floating in space.

### 4. Objects form adjacency chains

The accepted images repeatedly use chains such as:

- bed → reachable light → side surface/storage → curtain or wall layer
- seat → throw/cushion → light → small table → rug
- desk → task lamp → shelf/display → plant/window
- low shelf → books/personal objects → plant/art → adjacent activity

An object should usually connect to an activity and at least one nearby element. Do not add a chair, plant, or lamp solely to fill a gap.

### 5. Lighting is visible, local, multi-height, and time-specific

Pleated shades, paper lanterns, articulated lamps, floor lamps, and small table lamps appear as visible warm nodes. They create overlapping pools at floor, seated, bedside, and upper levels while allowing some shadow. The aesthetic layer comes from **where the light gathers**, not from turning the whole image orange.

Default to **bright warm mixed-light interiors**, following [lighting-model.md](lighting-model.md): soft neutral/gently cool exterior base light, broad diffuse warm ambient fill with believable bounce, distinct local activity pools, and open readable shadows. Warm lamps should shape the interior mood while daylight can support visibility. Judge warmth and brightness separately; do not substitute a yellow filter or tiny bright lamps for ambient coverage. Blue hour remains an optional requested treatment, not the default.

### 6. Warmth comes from tactile contrast

Successful rooms combine several tactile signals: rumpled or full bedding, woven rugs, curtains, throws, upholstered seating, wood grain, baskets, paper shades, ceramics, and leaves. They balance soft volumes with a few firm lines or dark masses so the image does not dissolve into a pale blur.

### 7. Palettes have roles, not just matching colors

Use a quiet background field, a coherent material/color family, one or more value anchors, and a small living accent. The collection supports several distinct families:

- **Earthy botanical collector:** brown/dark wood, olive or sage, cream textiles, plants, warm lamps — rooms 1, 5/8, 14, 16.
- **Young layered bookish:** navy or dark checks, mixed medium/dark wood, warm white, muted red/brown accents, books, toys or current-use objects, and irregular wall/floor layers — rooms 2, 4/9, 7, 16. Avoid combining matching dark wood, formal framed grids, heavy taupe curtains, traditional rug, and perfectly made plaid bedding as one complete set; that shortcut reads older and more formal than these examples.
- **Light natural retreat:** warm white, oat, pale wood, sage/green, woven fibers, rounded seating — rooms 6, 13, 14.
- **Graphic urban nest:** black, deep blue, gray, white, bold graphic art, sculptural stacks, warm localized light — room 3.
- **Paper-lantern eclectic:** warm white bedding, charcoal accent, pale/dark wood mix, several paper lights, plants, books, and small tables — rooms 12/17.

Choose one family that fits the source room and resident. Fixed surfaces are compatibility anchors, not commands. Do not randomly merge all families, default every customer to beige, or choose a restrained neutral route merely because it is safe.

For customer uploads, choose the family automatically from fixed surfaces and daylight rather than asking the customer:

- medium/dark wood floor → forest/natural or Chinese/American retro, selected from the fixed lines, wood tone and daylight;
- gray marble or glossy gray tile → black-white/cool restrained minimal, softened by textiles, localized warm light, active walls and greenery;
- pale wood or yellow-brown wood floor → cream/natural, forest/natural, or youthful layered bookish retro with dark checks, brown wood, muted red, plants and personal objects;
- mixed/neutral surfaces → follow the strongest fixed color/value anchor and the selected functions.

### 8. The vertical field is layered

Curtains, wall art, hanging plants, shelves, corkboards, mirrors, tall plant silhouettes, and lamps distribute interest from floor to ceiling. The strongest rooms avoid a furnished floor beneath blank upper walls, while also preserving some quiet wall area.

For customer renders, do not leave a major visible wall as an unexplained blank field. Give each visible wall a deliberate role through an irregular poster/photo group, felt or pin board, shelf, textile, hanging object, light, plant silhouette, or a smaller quiet interval between active clusters. “Activated” does not mean covering every square centimeter or using a formal gallery grid.

### 9. Greenery is a repeated living layer

For customer renders, include at least three visible plant placements unless the user opts out. Use varied scale and topology—for example one floor or large shelf plant, one medium daylight plant, and one small desk/bedside plant—rather than three identical pots in a row. Place them where light and care are plausible, connect them to different scene edges or height layers, and do not block windows, curtains, routes, work area, or bed access.

### 10. Identity is legible, accumulated, and edited

Books, a guitar, records, plants, a workstation, toys, collected art, and photographs imply a resident with interests. If the resident is unknown, build a coherent reversible story through hobby/collection, display, playful and active-use evidence. These are roles, not one-object quotas. Let related possessions recur at storage and use locations with varied states, scale and silhouette; never copy a creator's exact personal objects or invent customer biographical facts.

### 11. Lived-in cues are controlled by hierarchy

Natural bedding and one open book alone cannot establish the user's desired life density. Several activity-linked surfaces should carry stored, in-use and temporarily placed objects, with selective misalignment, varied stacking and partial visual overlap. Scene boundaries may remain functionally legible while collections and textiles visually extend across them. Use the activity-state map and density acceptance checks in [comfort-density.md](comfort-density.md); quantity without varied states is still a display, and random scattering is not a substitute.

### 12. The image has foreground, middle, and background

Rug edges, a chair back, low table, bed corner, or plant can create a modest foreground; the primary scene occupies the middle; window, curtain, wall, shelves, or art close the background. For multi-scene bedrooms, favor a triangular depth composition when the geometry permits: one large foreground anchor frames two different background scenes, so the viewer discovers work, rest, plants, lamps, and wall identity in sequence. The hero frame should maximize the desire to enter, not prove that every aisle is generous. A second close/detail frame may make the most inviting lounge or bedside corner tactile. Preserve room geometry, fixed openings, furniture layout, and believable lens perspective; avoid artificial ultra-wide expansion.

### 13. Silhouette contrast prevents coordinated formality

Rectangular beds, desks, windows, cabinets, and posters dominate most bedrooms. Break that geometry with a limited set of different silhouettes: a slouching beanbag or soft chair, rounded stool or shade, arched object, vertical instrument, irregular leaf canopy, draped curtain, or plush form. Echo the shapes across scenes without making them a matching collection. Color warmth alone cannot rescue a composition made entirely of crisp rectangles and parallel edges.

## Adapt the grammar to room capacity

- **Compact/small room:** provide sleep plus the customer's one selected fitting scene; emphasize a two-wall corner bed, vertical layers, reachable edges, and low furniture. Use a complete micro version when needed.
- **Medium room:** provide sleep plus the customer's two selected fitting scenes, using orientation, rug edges, low storage, or light rather than heavy dividers. Keep the bed at a wall corner unless the room is genuinely large.
- **Large room:** provide sleep plus the analyzed number of selected, fitting leisure, work, reading, bar, coffee, dressing, hobby, media, fitness, or window scenes.

If dimensions are unknown, infer conservatively from doors, windows, flooring, and known furniture, and avoid irreversible layout assumptions.

## Customer-image generation workflow

Use two reasoning passes and, when supported, one visual-reference pass before rendering:

1. **Transformation pass:** lock geometry and fixed openings; classify temporary obstruction and movable storage; compare current with recovered capacity; apply the bedroom zoning hierarchy; test wall/corner bed placement in compact/medium rooms; establish anchor orientation, complete activity chains, clusters, storage adjacency, boundaries, and minimum usable access. Use [activity-zoning.md](activity-zoning.md) for the required scene map.
2. **Aesthetic pass:** select one compatible but emotionally specific palette family; establish the dominant scene and secondary stops; create connected density transitions; assign local lights at useful heights; choose an intentional capture time and use the four-layer bright-warm lighting model by default; layer tactile materials, contrasting silhouettes and vertical surfaces; preserve meaningful active-use and resident-specific evidence; compose explicitly for a compelling vlog frame.
3. **Visual-reference pass:** if multiple image inputs are supported, select one capacity-matched reference using [style-reference-index.md](style-reference-index.md), optionally a second for one named missing quality. Label them as style references only, never edit targets.

Then render or write the editing prompt. Inspect the result against the gates below and revise only the highest-leverage failures.

## Acceptance gates for a generated image

### Hard realism gates

- Architecture, window/door placement, and room proportions remain recognizable. Camera position may improve, but it must depict the same geometry and furniture layout.
- Furniture scale is plausible; fixed doors/windows, ventilation, at least one bed entry, and the minimum use of every selected activity still work.
- Added scenes fit the room instead of making it look larger through invention or wide-angle distortion.
- Every named scene is minimally usable: a person can enter, adopt its posture, reach its resources and support, perform the activity, and leave. Compact sequential overlap and a bed that doubles as a lounge/daybed are valid.
- Relevant storage is reachable from the activity it serves; a source wardrobe may be removed or redistributed, and temporary clutter does not conceal obviously recoverable scene capacity.

### Aesthetic gates

- The hero frame passes the three-second invitation test: it makes the intended viewer want to enter and stay before they inspect practicality.
- A dominant scene is immediately readable.
- Secondary objects form activity-based clusters rather than a prop checklist.
- Visual density is concentrated at anchors and relieved along transitions.
- The interior has broad warm coverage and readable midtones, visible local pools and soft shadows, with gentle exterior contrast rather than compulsory blue hour. Assess bed, desk, lounge and foreground independently of the bright window; no orange wash or clipped lampshades.
- Texture and value contrast make the room feel tactile and deep.
- Rectangular architecture is softened by varied furniture, plant, textile, and personal-object silhouettes.
- The vertical field and foreground/middle/background are all considered.
- Identity cues form a coherent story distributed across relevant surfaces, not a sparse one-prop-per-role checklist.
- The room looks inhabited, emotionally absorbent, and believable; cleaning effort and frequent restyling are not aesthetic failures.
- Macro order, meso overlap, and micro irregularity are all present; the result does not look purchased, aligned, and completed in one sitting.
- Life density survives beyond bedding wrinkles and a token open book: compare active surfaces, temporary placement, selective overlap and scene connections with the chosen density reference. Avoid both all-items-returned-to-place order and uniformly scattered filler.
- Rugs and open floor have a legible role as activity support, connection, route, access, or intentional relief; they do not substitute for a missing scene.
- The render contains the required zoning tier, no avoidable centered-bed residual strips, no large unexplained blank wall field, and at least three plausible visible plant placements unless the user opted out.

If the image fails a hard gate, correct geometry/scale before aesthetic polishing. If hard gates pass but the result feels generic, revise the dominant scene, cluster relationships, density gradient, or lighting map before adding more props.

## Common generation failures

- Cozy-prop dumping: plants, candles, books, art, and pillows appear but do not create an activity.
- Beige wash: everything has the same pale value and texture, producing softness without depth.
- Uniform amber cast: white walls, daylight, and object colors are lost under a global warm filter.
- Flat lighting: either daylight alone or uniform overhead light erases local warm glow and scene hierarchy; daylight itself is not a fault.
- Dark light-islands: bright tiny lamps sit in an underexposed shell, with unreadable foreground/ceiling/floor and a saturated blue window. Improve warm ambient fill before adding more small lamps.
- Furniture showroom: pieces are individually attractive but isolated, centered, and unused.
- Reference overfit: a sofa, beanbag, guitar, or record player is copied into a room that cannot support it or a resident who did not request it.
- Style soup: several exemplar families are merged without a coherent palette or story.
- Generated clutter: small objects fill every shelf and surface with no hierarchy.
- Over-completion: every edge aligns, every surface is cleared, and bed/work/lounge scenes become separate matching furniture packages.
- Generic identity: a laptop, generic books, anonymous art and identical plants imply no particular life, hobby, playfulness, or active moment.
- Symbolic zoning: a rug, chair, bookcase, or lamp visually suggests an activity but the required posture, reach, surface, light, or access relationship is missing.
- Premature capacity reduction: temporary boxes, suitcases, or piles make the prompt forbid an auxiliary scene that would fit after functional noise is contained.
- Zoning shortfall: the room renders attractively but omits one of the customer's selected scenes or replaces it with a symbolic chair, rug, or storage element.
- Blank-shell warmth: floor props and textiles are added while major visible walls remain empty and emotionally detached.
- Token greenery: one generic plant is used as a symbolic cozy prop instead of three varied, plausible living placements.
- Formal nostalgia shortcut: matching dark wood, plaid bed, thick taupe curtains, traditional rug, pleated lamps, and framed grid combine into an older formal bedroom regardless of the requested mood.
- Architectural drift: windows, doors, ceiling height, or room width change to make the design easier; a changed viewpoint is acceptable only when it faithfully depicts the same layout.
