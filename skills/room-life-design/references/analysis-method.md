# Room-case analysis method

Use this method for reference collections, before/after studies, high-engagement finished-room exemplars, and updates to the evidence base.

## 1. Audit before interpretation

Create a manifest of folders, files, readable formats, dimensions, and obvious duplicates. Use contact sheets for side-by-side inspection. For every folder assign one evidence state:

- **Paired:** the same room has a defensible before and after.
- **After-only:** one or more finished views; describe the final-room mechanisms and visual grammar, but not unobserved changes.
- **Before-only:** diagnose potential, but do not claim results.
- **Ambiguous:** chronology or room identity cannot be verified.
- **Duplicate/supplement:** a repeated room or added angle belonging to another independent case.
- **Empty/unreadable:** no usable visual evidence.

Chronology may be supported by on-image labels, unmistakable shared architecture, or obvious furnished/unfurnished states. Filename sequence alone is weak evidence.

Record any provenance supplied by the user, including whether the example was deliberately curated from a high-engagement post or video. Do not invent or normalize exact engagement counts that are not present in the source data.

## 2. Analyze every independent room on the same grid

Record visible evidence before interpretation.

### A. Behavioral layer

- Primary life scene and any credible auxiliary scenes
- Actions visibly supported: sleep, read, work, dress, lounge, display, make music, care for plants
- Whether a “zone” is genuinely usable or only staged furniture

### B. Spatial layer

- Anchor placement and orientation
- Adjacency chains and complete clusters
- Boundary devices: shelf, rug, furniture back, curtain, lighting pool, change of orientation
- Circulation, doors, windows, bed access, storage access, and retained negative space
- Foreground/midground/background and low/mid/high visual layers

### C. Sensory layer

- Light topology: ambient, task, accent; uniformity versus pools and shadow
- Soft/hard material balance and tactile cues
- Color value structure, temperature, contrast, and dark anchors
- Acoustic or privacy cues when visibly supportable

### D. Identity and practical-cost layer

- Personal interests and specific possessions versus generic props
- Controlled irregularity versus either sterility or uncontrolled clutter
- Existing-object classification: active-use evidence, identity/collection, generic decoration, functional noise/hazard
- Macro order, meso-scale scene overlap, and micro-scale irregularity
- Safety impossibilities versus optional costs such as dust load, plant care, cleaning effort, ergonomics, and storage displacement. Record optional costs as evidence, but do not use them to reject a visually stronger product result unless the user asks for practicality.

### E. Capture and vlog-image layer

- Time of day, exposure, color cast, crop, viewpoint, staging, bedding arrangement and visible light sources. Record whether cool exterior/window ambience and warm interior pools create refuge, depth or scene separation. Separate architectural distortion from legitimate image-making: a new faithful viewpoint, blue-hour capture and deliberate styling are product tools, while artificial room widening remains invalid. Do not treat neutral daylight as an automatic advantage.

### F. Target-aesthetic layer

- Dominant scene and subordinate visual stops
- Asymmetrical balance and repeated motifs
- Density gradient: concentrated anchors versus quieter passages
- Palette roles, value depth, and material family
- Visible light nodes and their distribution by height
- Foreground/middle/background construction
- Whether identity cues form a coherent story or generic prop set
- Silhouette variety: rectangular shell and anchors versus soft/slouching, rounded, arched, vertical, leafy and draped interruptions
- Three-second invitation: whether the first frame makes the intended viewer want to enter and stay before analyzing practicality
- Which aesthetic family the room exemplifies and which source-room types it would suit

## 3. Paired-case delta

For true pairs, describe changes in this order:

1. Removed or retained obstacles
2. Reorientation of anchors
3. New or changed life scenes
4. New boundaries and adjacency chains
5. Circulation and access effects
6. Light, textile, color, and vertical-surface effects
7. Identity signals
8. Capture-time and viewpoint effects, distinguishing legitimate desired mood from geometry distortion
9. Tradeoffs and likely maintenance costs, clearly marked as non-blocking for a visual-first/vlog brief

Do not attribute the result to small props when layout, exposure, or a major furniture deletion explains more.

## 4. Evidence and confidence

Label important claims:

- **Observed:** directly visible.
- **Inferred:** likely but not proven from the view.
- **Recommendation:** depends on the target resident and constraints.

Do not collapse evidence into one confidence score. Label two axes:

- **Transformation evidence:** confidence in what changed and why the makeover improved.
- **Target-aesthetic evidence:** relevance as a desirable final-image target, strengthened by repeated patterns across independent curated examples and by user-supplied engagement provenance.

Use confidence descriptively within the relevant axis:

- **High transformation evidence:** repeated across independent paired cases or directly demonstrated by a strong pair.
- **High target-aesthetic evidence:** a curated/high-engagement finished image that clearly demonstrates a repeated, coherent visual pattern, even without a before view.
- **Medium:** a sound spatial or visual mechanism with limited repetition, uncertain provenance, or notable capture confounding.
- **Low:** ambiguous room identity, unreadable image, or a claim that depends on hidden information.

After-only cases can strongly support “a well-received finished room uses this compositional grammar”; they cannot establish “adding X caused the improvement”. Paired and finished-only cases therefore answer different questions and should be combined, not ranked on a single ladder.

## 5. Cross-case synthesis

Build two matrices with independent rooms as rows.

The **transformation matrix** is driven mainly by paired cases. Useful columns include:

- scene credibility
- cluster completeness
- boundary strength
- circulation preservation
- light topology
- vertical activation
- soft-material layering
- color/value depth
- visual-height variation
- identity specificity
- controlled irregularity
- over-completion or matching-set formality
- meaningful comfort density versus functional noise
- maintenance cost
- photographic confounding

The **target-aesthetic matrix** includes every curated finished room. Useful columns include:

- dominant scene clarity
- secondary visual stops
- asymmetrical balance
- density gradient
- adjacency-chain quality
- palette/material family
- light-node topology
- tactile layering
- vertical-field activation
- foreground/middle/background
- identity coherence
- silhouette contrast
- cool-window/warm-interior lighting tension
- three-second invitation strength
- controlled irregularity
- compatibility with small, medium, or large rooms
- audience-engagement provenance

Compare cases against existing principles and aesthetic patterns and mark **support / narrow / contradict / not tested**. Report both the dominant pattern and the best counterexample. A transformation rule should become more conditional when the counterexample is credible; a minority aesthetic can remain a valid style family rather than being averaged away.

## 6. Update record

When incorporating new cases, update the evidence base with:

- version and date
- raw folder/image count
- independent-room count
- paired, after-only, ambiguous, duplicate, empty counts
- new case notes
- principles promoted, narrowed, or challenged
- aesthetic patterns and compatible style families promoted, narrowed, or challenged
- engagement provenance supplied by the user
- unresolved questions and missing views

Keep source-specific observations separate from general design rules so future samples can revise the latter without erasing the former.
