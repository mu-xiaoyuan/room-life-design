# Activity zoning: capacity and functional closure

Read this reference for target-room planning, customer-image generation, or render critique whenever the number, placement, or usability of life scenes is in question.

## Judge recovered capacity, not photographed congestion

Evaluate the room twice:

1. **Current occupied state:** fixed architecture, furniture that must remain, active-use objects, identity/collection, generic decoration, and functional noise.
2. **Recovered state:** the plausible room after temporary packaging, suitcases, unstable piles, redundant furniture, blocked-route items, and other functional noise are removed, contained, or rerouted.

Do not reduce the room program merely because temporary storage makes the photograph look small. Movable wardrobes and storage are design variables, not protected architecture: remove, replace, or redistribute them when that produces a stronger life scene. Conversely, do not infer extra capacity from a wide-angle image. Use doors, windows, known furniture, flooring modules, and basic human posture as conservative scale evidence.

## Allocate activities before decorative zones

For each requested or plausible activity:

1. Name the activity and body posture: sleeping, desk work, upright reading, reclining, dressing, music, or storage retrieval.
2. Assign its largest anchor: bed, desk, seat, wardrobe, instrument, or work surface.
3. Attach the resources needed during the activity.
4. Reserve the minimum body posture and entry/use/exit sequence; allow sequential overlap with a route or adjacent scene.
5. Connect it to the main route without letting that route become an empty stage.

## Default bedroom zoning hierarchy

For customer-facing bedroom plans and renders, apply this user-defined product policy after the recovered-capacity audit:

- **Every bedroom:** a complete sleep scene is mandatory.
- **Compact/small bedroom:** after reserving the bed, fixed access and a minimally workable route, choose one auxiliary scene that actually fits, defaulting to leisure unless customer requirements or visible activities favor another.
- **Medium bedroom:** sleep plus two complete auxiliary scenes may fit; without preferences, test independent leisure and work before choosing fewer scenes.
- **Large bedroom:** add only fitting scenes that support the brief and composition; do not fill capacity with arbitrary specialist functions.

Honor explicit customer choices. Choose functions automatically for photo-only makeover requests; ask a short function question only when the customer requests a guided choice, following [customer-intake.md](customer-intake.md).

Use operational capacity rather than asking for dimensions. If no bed is visible, reserve 1.8 m × 2.0 m before classifying capacity; otherwise use the visible bed footprint. A compact/small room is one where sleep plus one closed auxiliary scene fit but a third scene would block access or essential storage. A medium room can close sleep plus two auxiliary scenes. A large room has usable capacity beyond that.

Possible specialist scenes include:

- **focused reading:** supportive chair, reachable books, task light, side surface;
- **dressing/grooming:** wardrobe or vanity, mirror, standing/seat space, lighting and landing point;
- **hobby/creative:** instrument, easel, craft or making surface, tool/material storage;
- **media/music:** listening or viewing posture, equipment, media storage and controlled light;
- **fitness/stretch:** genuinely usable open body envelope, mat/equipment storage and ventilation;
- **window pause/tea:** view-facing seat, cup/book surface and local light;
- **pet or childcare:** only when the resident brief explicitly requires it.

Storage/display alone is a support function, not a counted life scene. One physical anchor may support more than one scene: a wall-aligned bed can be both sleep and lounge/daybed, and a substantial desk can support work plus reading or creative activity when the posture, light, and resources make each mode explicit. Scene count is not furniture count.

## Bed placement and space recovery

Do not inherit the photographed bed orientation by default. Preserve an existing bed's apparent mattress size/type, then explicitly compare every visibly plausible candidate: the photographed position, both 90-degree axes, a 180-degree head/foot reversal, sliding along each viable wall in both directions, every two-wall corner, and a centered/floating position only for a genuinely large room. Eliminate candidates that block fixed doors/windows, ventilation or air-conditioning, or make all bed entry impossible. One-sided bed entry and touching two walls are acceptable and often preferred; cleaning convenience is not a rejection criterion. A movable wardrobe may be removed or replaced rather than forcing an inferior bed position.

In ordinary bedrooms, prefer the surviving candidate with the head and one long side against two walls when it consolidates the remaining floor into a useful continuous footprint rather than two narrow residual strips. Score the **residual-corner yield**: which candidate releases a corner or wall segment that can close a whole selected scene, not merely increase visible floor. A bed may stay off a second wall only when the corner alternatives fail a fixed-access gate or reduce the selected scene's real usability.

## Independent scenes before compound shortcuts

When the customer selects two or more auxiliary functions, first attempt to give each one an independent, visually complete micro-scene. Do not count a bed as leisure merely because it can be sat on, and do not merge work and reading merely because both involve a chair.

Before declaring that an independent scene will not fit:

1. rerun the full bed search, including head/foot reversal and wall-axis slides;
2. remove or redistribute visually heavy movable storage;
3. reduce the furniture envelope without deleting the activity—a shallow desk, armless push-under wood chair, low open cubbies, beanbag/floor chair, small footstool, or narrow side table may close the scene;
4. test whether two use envelopes can share a passage sequentially while their anchors remain independent.

Use a compound bed/daybed lounge only when all plausible independent candidates fail, when the customer selects only leisure and the bed-centered nest is emotionally stronger, or when the user explicitly prefers bed-centered life. A compound scene must still have an intentional reclining posture, reachable surface, local light, soft support, and activity/identity resources.

## Candidate layout comparison

Do not stop at the first composition that appears to contain the requested furniture. Before rendering, compare the viable candidates in this order:

1. **Hard fixed-geometry gates:** architecture, doors/windows, air flow and fixed equipment remain believable; at least one bed entry remains possible.
2. **Minimum activity plausibility:** the resident can approach, adopt the posture, operate the anchor and leave. Sequential sharing of the same passage or chair-pull area is allowed; simultaneous circulation is not required.
3. **Functional closure:** resources, support, light/power and relevant storage are reachable from the activity.
4. **Emotional and visual value:** prefer the candidate with the strongest connected life system, foreground-to-background depth, personal identity, and vlog-ready composition.
5. **Recovered-space quality and convenience:** use as tie-breakers only. Compact passages, one-sided bed access, and higher upkeep do not defeat a visually stronger candidate.

For visual estimation without customer measurements, use the visible bed, known furniture proportions and human posture as scale anchors. Approximate ergonomic dimensions are advisory priors, not pass/fail thresholds. A compact desk still needs visible knee space and a believable chair posture, but its retreat area may share a route sequentially and may be tighter than commercial recommendations. Unless long-hour ergonomic work is explicitly requested, prefer the smallest believable furniture shell and the strongest visual integration over a conventional rolling office chair, armrests, or generous retreat space. Reject only placements where the body cannot plausibly enter, sit, use the desk, and exit; otherwise let visual and emotional value decide.

## Scene-closure test

A named scene exists only when a person can plausibly enter it, adopt the required posture, reach its resources, perform the activity, and leave. Minor repositioning of a light object and sequential sharing of compact space are acceptable; moving the bed or dismantling the scene is not.

Check six components:

- **posture/seat:** where the body actually goes;
- **anchor:** the main furniture or equipment;
- **resource:** books, devices, clothing, tools, or media used there;
- **support:** a reachable surface or storage point for objects in hand;
- **light/power:** appropriate illumination and power when the activity needs them;
- **use envelope:** access, occupied body space, chair movement, reaching and exit. It may overlap a route sequentially when both uses remain plausible; cleaning clearance is not required.

The exact objects vary, but the relationship must close. Examples:

- reading: supportive seat or intentional bed posture → reachable books → reading light → landing surface → comfortable leg and access space;
- leisure: a relaxed chair, floor posture, or explicitly styled daybed/bed posture → reachable side/low surface → soft support or media/hobby object → local light → believable access;
- work: unobstructed approach → desk → movable chair with occupied and retreat positions → tools/screen → task light and power → storage access → unobstructed exit;
- sleep: bed → at least one usable entry edge → reachable light → bedside landing/storage → ventilation and curtain clearance;
- dressing: clothing storage → opening clearance → standing/viewing space → hamper or landing point.

Do not count a label, rug, chair, or bookcase as a complete scene by itself.

## Resource adjacency

Place storage according to the activity it serves, not where it improves the composition most.

- A bookcase meant for reading belongs within a short, unobstructed reach or walk of the reading seat, with space to browse lower shelves.
- Books used for work may belong beside the desk instead; bedtime books may belong at a reachable bed edge.
- A movable desk chair does not automatically double as a reading chair when its orientation, retreat space, light, or ergonomics keep it tied to the desk.
- If one storage element serves two scenes, both access paths must work and neither scene may block the other.

## Rugs, open floor, and false zones

A rug is a boundary or connector, not an activity. It should catch meaningful furniture edges, support a posture, connect adjacent scenes, or mark a real route. A large rug with no usable seat, table, bed edge, or movement purpose is an **empty rug stage** and often signals a missing scene.

Negative space is valid when it protects fixed access, a real activity, or deliberate visual relief. Label its purpose. If the largest recovered area has no such purpose, test whether it should support an auxiliary scene or whether furniture orientation is wasting capacity. Do not preserve a large empty rug stage merely to make the room look spacious.

## Pre-render allocation check

Before writing the image prompt, make a compact internal candidate comparison followed by a scene map:

| Candidate | Bed orientation/corner | Fixed-geometry gates | Minimum activity use | Emotional composition | Pass? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| Scene | Posture/anchor | Resources + support | Light/power | Use envelope | Complete? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Reject impossible candidates before choosing one. Then account for every major floor area as one of: scene footprint, shared transition, route, use envelope, or intentional relief. Do not render until the proposed scene count and the floor allocation agree. Do not count a desk as an office when the desk rectangle fits but no plausible seated body/entry/exit exists.

For bedroom renders, the scene map must confirm the inferred bed footprint/corner orientation and that sleep plus every customer-selected auxiliary scene is closed.

## Post-render rejection checks

Revise the render when any of these occur:

- the prompt names three scenes but the image contains only two usable ones;
- a compact/small bedroom lacks the customer's selected auxiliary scene, or a medium bedroom omits either of the customer's two selected auxiliary scenes;
- a centered bed creates two narrow residual strips while a wall/corner orientation could consolidate a complete auxiliary scene;
- the candidate search omits a head/foot reversal or a wall-axis slide that would release a complete residual corner;
- a selected independent lounge or work scene is collapsed into the bed before slimmer furniture and alternate bed placements are tested;
- a bulky conventional office chair or deep desk consumes the only viable auxiliary corner even though a shallow desk and push-under chair would close the activity;
- the photographed bed orientation was preserved without comparing a rotation or another viable wall corner;
- a desk is visible but no person can plausibly approach, sit, work and exit;
- storage is visually present but cannot be approached, opened, browsed, or used from its intended scene;
- the only nearby seat belongs functionally to another activity;
- a large rug or empty floor patch substitutes for a lounge, reading, or work scene;
- an auxiliary scene exists only as a decorative chair, lamp, or table without the rest of its relationship chain;
- removing temporary clutter reveals enough safe capacity for a requested scene, but the design leaves that capacity purposeless;
- adding a scene blocks fixed openings, all bed access, ventilation, or the minimum posture/entry/exit needed for another selected activity.

Correct bed/anchor orientation and activity adjacency before adding decorative objects. If another bed candidate would recover a complete selected scene, rerender that layout. Once minimum use is credible, prefer the candidate with greater emotional density and visual storytelling rather than the one with the widest empty passage.
